B2
The Instrument · The Legal Posture Your AG Will Test First

The state is a revenue counterparty, not a purchaser.

This is the load-bearing structural fact of the entire engagement, and it is what removes the three obstacles that normally kill a technology partnership at the state level: competitive bid, appropriation, and vendor-risk exposure. No state funds change hands in either direction except inbound.

THE STATE COMMITS TO FOUR THINGS
  • · Name one pilot corridor. One industry vertical and three flagship public institutions. Houston Energy and NYC Finance are the modeled reference corridors.
  • · Designate a settlement recipient. One Treasury account or dedicated workforce fund of record to receive the 20% dividend.
  • · Appoint a governance liaison. One named officer — typically from the Workforce Commission or the Governor's policy office — to hold the Kill-Switch Dashboard credential.
  • · Permit institutional participation. A board-level acknowledgment that the named public institutions may execute their own Genesis MOUs. Not a mandate.
TENURED AI COMMITS TO FOUR THINGS
  • · Both settlements. 20% of the per-semester Access Fee at public institutions, and 20% of every in-state transaction in perpetuity — two separate instruments, both routed atomically at the smart-contract layer, both unrestricted as to use. Detailed at §B5.
  • · Zero cost to the state — no license, no implementation fee, no consulting engagement, no matching requirement, no in-kind commitment.
  • · Kill-Switch Dashboard and AICI Heatmap provisioned to the designated office at go-live, covering every participating institution.
  • · Full audit access — Merkle-proof verification of every routed dollar, independently confirmable without trusting the platform.

Not a procurement

No state funds are obligated, so no competitive-bid statute attaches. The MOU is a revenue-participation instrument of the kind states already execute for royalty, settlement, and licensing income.

Not a mandate

The pilot does not compel any institution to participate or any student to enroll. Adoption is institution-by-institution and student-by-student. Statewide mandate is a Phase 2 legislative option, not a Phase 1 requirement.

Not a vendor dependency

Credentials anchor on public chain via Merkle root. If the platform ceased operating tomorrow, every credential issued to a citizen of your state remains independently verifiable, and every routed dollar remains in your Treasury.

Why atomic settlement matters to Treasury

There is no counterparty holding your money

Conventional revenue-share arrangements accrue at a vendor and reconcile quarterly — which means the state is an unsecured creditor for ninety days at a time. Here the split executes at the contract layer at the instant of transaction. The platform never holds the state's share, so it cannot fail to remit it.

Why the audit posture matters to the AG

Verification does not require trust

Every routed dollar carries a Merkle proof anchored to public chain. Your Comptroller reconciles against the chain, not against a statement we produce. This is the difference between an audit of our books and an audit of the ledger — and it is the answer to the "what if they under-report" question before it is asked.

Revenue characteristics
  • · Unrestricted. Not federal pass-through, not matched, not earmarked by us
  • · Non-tax. Transaction-derived, not levied on residents
  • · Counter-cyclical. Tied to enrollment, which rises in downturns
  • · Earmarkable by statute. Your legislature may direct it — workforce, K-12 STEM, community college
B5
The Economics · Two Different 40/40/20s

Same ratio. Different instruments. Do not conflate them.

Onboarding is the decision; the splits are the arithmetic that follows. There are two, and because both divide 40/40/20 they are routinely treated as one thing in briefing — including by people who have read the deck. They are not the same money. The Access Split divides a per-student enrollment fee. The Dividend Split divides a per-hire transaction. Treasury receives 20% of each.

The distinction in one line

The Access Split divides the $100 Sovereign Access Fee charged per enrolled student, per semester at participating public institutions. Termly. Scales with public headcount. Settles whether or not a single graduate has yet been hired.

The Dividend Split divides the transaction value a graduate generates in the labor market. Perpetual. Scales with corridor employment. Begins once cleared graduates enter the recruiter pipeline.

THE DOUBLE-DIP

The Treasury receives the settlement and the widened tax base. Verified graduates in the modeled corridors clear $15K–$20K above national median in first-role compensation. The income-tax and sales-tax effect of that delta, compounded across a graduating cohort, is a second-order return the settlement figures above do not include.

All figures are modeled projections against platform underwriting economics and stated enrollment inputs. They are not booked revenue, not a guarantee, and not a forecast of your state's specific outcome. Full model available under specific-purpose NDA.

Instrument 01

Live talent pipe for site selection

Your economic development office can show a prospective employer the exact count of verified graduates in a named competency cluster within a fifty-mile radius — not a projection from degree conferrals, a live count of cleared Hard-Gates. This changes what an incentive negotiation can be built on.

Instrument 02

Brain-drain early warning

The Alumni Continuity Protocol tracks the share of certified graduates remaining in-state, by corridor and by institution. When a corridor's retention rate breaks trend, the office sees it in the quarter it happens — early enough to deploy a targeted credit or housing incentive rather than commissioning a study after the fact.

Instrument 03

Institutional ROI leaderboard

Institutions ranked by skill velocity — movement from Apprentice to Practitioner — and by verified artifact output, not by enrollment or by spend. This gives the state a defensible basis for allocating scaling grants to institutions that are actually producing, and a defensible basis for declining to.

Privacy floor

Every view in the Heatmap is aggregate and k-anonymity preserving. The state never sees an individual student, an individual score, or an individual employment record — at any zoom level, under any filter, for any office.

B7
The Second-Order Return · The State Talent Credit

A ranking mechanism that pressures your non-participating institutions for you.

The state does not have to mandate adoption. The proposed State Innovation Ecosystem ranking category does the work — because a state-verified competency ledger is a data source the ranking bodies do not currently have from anyone.

THE MECHANISM

A credit that lifts every school in the state

A state operating a verified AICI ledger across its public system earns a State Talent Credit — a multi-factor input that raises the ranking position of every participating institution in that state, because their outcome data is state-audited rather than self-reported.

THE PRESSURE

Non-participation becomes visible

When the flagship in your corridor is running verified Hard-Gates and the peer institution across the state is not, the ranking differential appears without the state issuing a single directive. Adoption spreads through competitive pressure rather than through mandate — which is a materially easier political object.

THE PROTECTION

A shield for your public system

Public institutions are structurally disadvantaged in rankings that weight selectivity and per-student spend. A competency-verified input is the first ranking factor in a generation that rewards what a public system is actually good at — moving students from Apprentice to Practitioner at scale.

The State Talent Credit is a proposal under discussion with ranking bodies, not a committed methodology change. It is presented here as a modeled second-order return, and the pilot economics at §B5 do not depend on it.

B8
Statutory Pathways · Pilot, Then Scale

Three routes to statewide. The pilot needs none of them.

Phase 1 requires only an executive MOU. Phase 2 — moving from a three-institution corridor to a system-wide standard — has three viable paths, and the right one depends on your legislative calendar and your system governance structure, not on us.

PathInstrumentTimelineTrade-off
Path A
System board resolution		 A resolution of the university system's governing board adopting the AICI standard across constituent institutions. No legislature involved. One board cycle
60–120 days		 Fastest and lowest-friction. Binds only that system — a state with multiple independent systems needs multiple resolutions.
Path B
Appropriations rider		 A rider directing the Workforce Dividend into a named fund and conditioning a portion of institutional formula funding on participation. One session
6–14 months		 Most durable and hardest to reverse. Requires floor time and exposes the program to the appropriations negotiation.
Path C
Executive workforce order		 An executive order under existing workforce-development authority directing participating agencies and public institutions to adopt verified competency standards. 30–60 days
immediate effect		 Fast and unilateral. Reversible by a successor administration, so best paired with Path A or B in the following cycle.
Sequencing recommendation

Run the ninety-day pilot under executive MOU with no statutory instrument at all. At Month 12, the first settlement and twelve months of Heatmap data exist. Take Path A or B into the following session with a live revenue line and a live governance dashboard rather than a projection. The pilot is not a step toward the legislation — it is the evidence that makes the legislation trivial.
