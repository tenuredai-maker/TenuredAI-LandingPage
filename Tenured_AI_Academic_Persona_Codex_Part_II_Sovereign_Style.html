TAI-PC-003-II / 01Frontmatter · The Differentiator

THE HUMANITIES MANDATE

Part I mapped the STEM, finance, and applied-science majors who already speak the language of physical constraint. Part II takes on the harder, more valuable problem: proving that a Sociology, English, History, Journalism, or Philosophy student can drive tangible enterprise metrics — and that the non-technical management, teaching, and writing professions can orchestrate AI at scale without ever touching code.

The strategic thesis is blunt. In enterprise contexts, liberal-arts degrees carry a "fluff" tax — an assumption that the holder cannot connect their discipline to a manufactured product, a supply chain, or a physical liability. This volume weaponizes the platform against that assumption. Every one of these 80 archetypes is forced, by its domain's Tier Injection, to anchor its competency to physical reality: not the abstract analysis of a community, but the placement of its water-filtration hardware; not creative copy, but the material cost of a mis-printed hardware manual; not the theory of an ethical kill-switch, but its mechanical override specification.

The result is a differentiator no résumé can replicate. A humanities graduate carrying a Sovereign Passport from this track has done something their transcript cannot claim — they have proven, under adversarial pressure inside the Feasibility Filter, that their discipline produces tangible value. The two domain groups below reflect two distinct proofs: Liberal Arts & Humanities (Tiers XI–XV), which grounds classical disciplines in hardware; and Non-Technical & Enterprise Orchestration (Tiers XVI–XVIII), which proves teaching, consulting, and writing professionals can command multi-agent AI systems.

Continuity With Part I

This volume continues the sequence and the structure of TAI-PC-003. Global numbering resumes at 101 and runs to 180. Each domain still carries a Tier Injection — the discipline-bound Proving Ground evaluator — running beneath the same Universal Base Prompt. The archetypes here are rendered at the same full depth as Part I — each with its Major / Minor / Passion, Core Academic Drive, and Platform Augmentation Strategy — so a humanities student sees exactly which platform mechanics build their Passport.

TAI-PC-003-II / 02System · Non-Technical Scoring Architecture

THE RECALIBRATED MARKS

The non-technical tiers introduced here operate under a distinct scoring matrix. For students who never enter the developer track, the AIOI™ mark is read as an orchestration and project-management index, and the Builder Score is set aside. This is a deliberate calibration, not a contradiction of the Score Compendium — it is how the same marks apply to a professional who manages AI rather than building it.

AICI™
Competency Index · The Baseline

Unchanged from the Score Compendium: the baseline mark measuring the ability to use AI as a cognitive partner — safely, ethically, and with precision. In these tiers the same competency is demonstrated through physical-world problems, but the mark itself is never redefined as physical anchoring.

AIOI™
Orchestration Index · Project Management

Recalibrated for the non-technical track: measures the ability to manage multi-agent systems, oversee AI workflows, orchestrate digital processes, and handle complex project logistics — commanding AI, not coding it.

AIOI-ED™
Educator Designation

The strict sub-designation for teaching, administrative, and educational capacities — measuring pedagogical deployment and learning-loop management. Every Tier XVI evaluation routes here.

AIBS™
Builder Score · Excluded (Opt-In)

Excluded from these baseline evaluations. Reserved strictly as an opt-in metric for users who choose the technical developer track, so a non-technical Passport is never penalized for a mark it was never meant to carry.

Why This Matters

The recalibration touches only AIOI™ and AIBS™ — never AICI™. AICI™ remains exactly the base-competency mark defined in the Score Compendium: the ability to use AI as a cognitive partner, safely and precisely. What changes across these tiers is the arena in which that competency is proven — a humanities or orchestration student demonstrates the same core fluency by solving physical-world problems rather than abstract ones. A management consultant's Passport should read high on AIOI™ (they orchestrate agent swarms across a supply chain) and solid on AICI™ (base fluency, proven against physical constraint), with no AIBS™ gap held against them. The marks bend to the profession; the definition of AICI™ does not.
